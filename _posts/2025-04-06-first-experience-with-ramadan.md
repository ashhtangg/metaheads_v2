### Introduction

This year, I decided to partake in Ramadan. Now that my wife and I finally have our own space, she mentioned wanting to properly give it a try.

We ended up completing the entire month together, and surprisingly, I found it to be a rewarding experience. Initially, I assumed it would be a month of survival, but I came out of it with a clearer mind.

This being my first Ramadan, I thought it would be interesting for me to note down my experience so I can come back and reflect on it in the future.

### Adapting

The first few days were definitely difficult. It wasn't so much the food; however, the lack of water made it tough — especially during weekdays when I would often be talking to coworkers or on the phone.

The good news was that iftar (when you break fast) was quite early since the sun was setting around 6–7 p.m., so we were able to eat shortly after we commuted back home.

It felt both exciting and rewarding to finally come home and cook — to finally get the first sip of water or the first mouthful of hot food.

After the first 1–2 weeks, it became a routine. It's surprising how quickly your body is able to adapt.

### Doing More with Less

I watched a fair amount of Andrew Huberman. I went through a phase of being really interested in these "protocols" and how to have an optimal morning/routine.

An "optimal" routine goes out the window when you're not able to drink or eat until 6–7 p.m. in the afternoon.

I usually wake up around 6:30 to code for an hour before work. I thought that my mind would be noisier or less focused; however, like clockwork, I would just get up and study like I usually do.

During work, I thought that I would crash out; however, I seemed to be keeping up the same tempo as I would otherwise.

It reminded me that I was less delicate than I thought. That even with less resources, I can still bring myself to output a good amount of work. 

Coming out of Ramadan reminded me that the most important thing is to just sit down, lace up, and do what you’ve got to do.

### Community and Struggle

It's often said that people bond through doing things together. I think there’s something unique about struggling through a challenge together.

It's not about being good at a task; it's about both parties choosing to put continuous effort over a long period of time.

One of my close friends is Muslim, and we would often message each other throughout the month — checking in on how the fast was going or sharing what we had for iftar. We’d exchange tips about whether waking up early for suhoor (the pre-dawn meal) was worth it or discuss what helped us stay energized during the day.

My wife and I would often text about how good our coworkers’ lunches smelled or how tempting certain restaurant meals looked as we passed by them during our commute home.

When we broke our fast together, it was a special moment — to be able to eat with someone who you know has struggled in the same way that you have.

### Gratitude and Eid

At the end of Ramadan comes Eid — a celebration with family and friends to mark the end of fasting. My wife and I spent Eid with her side of the family.

These days, as everyone becomes busier with their own lives, it’s rare to get everyone together in one place. However, Eid seems to be one of those occasions where most family members turn up and celebrate together.

The dining table was laid out full of different home-cooked dishes brought by various family members, and my wife’s grandparents never stopped topping up my plate. It was nice to finally eat during the day but more importantly spend time with family.

As life becomes more complex and serious in adulthood, moments like these become increasingly precious. Eid allowed me the day to just relax and spend time with family - which I'm grateful for.
